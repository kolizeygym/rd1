The development of art appreciation in Melbourne, Australia

In the 1850s the British colony of Victoria in Australia was growing very quickly as more people came to live there. In general, the fine arts were not considered to be important at this time. This was partly because there was a gold rush that was catching people’s attention far more as it was bringing wealth to the people of Victoria. However, by the late 1850s, enthusiasm for the fine arts and for a public art museum was beginning to strengthen. In the city of Melbourne established artists, such as Thomas Clark and Nicholas Chevalier, were trying to make a living from their art and journalists like James Smith were becoming known as cultural critics. When the Melbourne Public Library opened in 1856, it was always expected that the library would eventually house a public art museum. Frederick McCoy, professor of natural science at the University of Melbourne, promoted the idea when he suggested placing a museum, focused on the education of citizens about art, on the ground floor of the Public Library.

From the beginning, there were opposing views about the public art museum’s role. Some people thought that the most effective way to educate people in art appreciation would be through demonstrating the history and development of art over the centuries whereas others felt that it would be better to display original paintings. In 1858, the Public Library trustees applied to the government for land at the rear of the library with the intention of building a museum of art. The application was approved and when the trustees decided that original art works would be too expensive, precise instructions were given for the purchase of a number of good quality reproductions of paintings and sculptures. It was felt that this would be the best way to illustrate the history of art and culture, and provide diverse examples from which local artists and artisans could learn. Learning about classical art in a museum of art was considered to be a transformative experience in art appreciation for individuals and society as a whole.

The Museum of Art was opened to the public on 24 May 1861. The Sculpture Gallery, although crammed into a limited space in the Museum of Art, revealed the Museum’s desire for the exhibits to have an educational role. Instead of genuine sculptures, casts reproducing sculptures were obtained and grouped chronologically, that is beginning with examples from ancient times and working through the eras to contemporary exhibits, in order to illustrate the historical development of art. Visitors to the Museum of Art learned about individual works by reading the written information on cards accompanying each object.

Enthusiasm grew for a new picture gallery; this was partly as a result of the 1861 Victoria Exhibition in Melbourne and the 1862 International Exhibition (or London Exhibition) which, in their displays, gave greater recognition to local artists. This resulted in a greater public interest in art exhibitions and in 1863 the government of Victoria gave one thousand pounds for the creation of a public picture gallery which was to display examples of contemporary art. This new public picture gallery soon became known as the National Gallery and was free to visit. In order to maximise the number of visitors, it was open in the evenings. Since the art collection was considered to be public property, it was decided that the public’s care and attention would safeguard it from theft and damage. To address concerns that the pictures were only available to the people of Melbourne, the Commission planned to circulate the improving and elevating influences of art throughout Victoria by lending works for exhibition in country towns.

From October 1866 to February 1867, an Intercolonial Exhibition was held in a temporary exhibition building erected in the library’s grounds. The picture collection was moved from the National Gallery for the duration of the exhibition. Almost half the total population of Victoria visited the exhibition. Such a large display of art works, mostly by Australian artists, broadened public interest in art produced in Australia. The publicity given to their work encouraged local artists, and the opportunity to compare and evaluate the works of local artists enhanced art criticism. After consideration, the role of the public art museum in Victoria was given a clearer definition and in 1870 changes were made that reflected the acceptance of a new attitude to art. The Museum of Art’s educational casts of sculptures were no longer on display but instead were relegated to the National Gallery’s new Schools of Design and Painting for instructing art students. Art works that were valued for their educational purpose, rather than solely their visual appeal, were placed in a separate study room. No longer encouraging public taste and knowledge through classical art, the National Gallery would value original paintings and encourage a love of art through the display of exhibits that reflected the art people liked and valued.
